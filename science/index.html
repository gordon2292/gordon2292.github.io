01

Homeostasis and feedback loops

The central idea for this unit

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within safe limits. Conditions still change slightly around a normal range, so homeostasis is dynamic rather than perfectly fixed.

StimulusSensorControl centreEffectorResponse

Negative feedback

The response counteracts the original change. It moves the condition back towards its normal range.

Examples: body temperature and blood glucose.

Positive feedback

The response increases the original change. The cycle continues until a clear endpoint stops it.

Examples: blood clotting and childbirth.

Common correction

In negative feedback, the initial stimulus is reduced or stopped. It is not amplified.

02

Endotherms, ectotherms and thermoregulation

How organisms control temperature

Endotherm

An animal that produces most of its body heat through metabolism. Mammals and birds can keep a fairly constant internal temperature, but this requires more food energy.

Ectotherm

An animal whose body temperature depends more on external heat. Reptiles and amphibians often move between warmer and cooler places to control temperature.

When an endotherm becomes too hot

  1. Stimulus: body temperature rises above the normal range.
  2. Sensor: thermoreceptors in the skin and hypothalamus detect the rise.
  3. Control centre: the hypothalamus coordinates a response.
  4. Effectors: sweat glands release sweat and skin arterioles widen.
  5. Response: evaporation and increased heat loss lower body temperature.

When an endotherm becomes too cold

The hypothalamus activates skeletal muscles and skin arterioles. Shivering produces heat, while vasoconstriction reduces heat loss from the skin.

Use the right term

Vasodilation means wider skin arterioles. Vasoconstriction means narrower skin arterioles.

03

The endocrine system

Hormones and target cells

The endocrine system is made of glands that secrete hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones travel through the body, but only target cells with the matching receptor respond.

SignalHormone
RouteBloodstream
SpeedUsually slower than nerves
EffectOften lasts longer

Glands to recognise

Pituitary gland
Releases hormones that control other endocrine glands. The hypothalamus helps regulate pituitary activity.
Thyroid gland
Produces hormones that affect metabolic rate.
Adrenal glands
Release hormones involved in the stress response.
Pancreas
Uses insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose.

Nervous control

Electrical impulses move along neurons. Responses are usually rapid and short.

Endocrine control

Hormones move in blood. Responses are usually slower and can last longer.

04

Blood glucose regulation

Insulin, glucagon and the pancreas

Blood glucose regulation is a negative feedback process. The pancreas acts as a sensor and control centre.

After a carbohydrate-rich meal

High blood glucose is detected by beta cells in the pancreas. They secrete insulin. Body cells take up more glucose, and the liver converts glucose to glycogen. Blood glucose falls towards its normal range.

Between meals or during exercise

Low blood glucose is detected by alpha cells in the pancreas. They secrete glucagon. The liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose. Blood glucose rises towards its normal range.

GlucoseA simple sugar used in respiration
GlycogenThe storage form of glucose
HyperglycaemiaBlood glucose is too high
HypoglycaemiaBlood glucose is too low

Type 1 diabetes

The pancreas makes little or no insulin because insulin-producing cells have been destroyed.

Type 2 diabetes

Target cells respond less effectively to insulin, and insulin production may also decrease.

05

Nervous system and reflexes

Fast communication through neurons

The nervous system detects internal or external changes. The central nervous system processes the information before coordinating a response.

Central nervous system

The brain and spinal cord. It receives information and coordinates responses.

Peripheral nervous system

The nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. It carries signals to and from the CNS.

Neuron roles

Sensory neuron
Carries an impulse from a receptor towards the CNS. It is also called an afferent neuron.
Relay neuron
Connects neurons inside the CNS. It is also called an interneuron.
Motor neuron
Carries an impulse away from the CNS to an effector. It is also called an efferent neuron.
Effector
A muscle or gland that produces the response.

Withdrawal reflex

Pain receptorSensory neuronRelay neuronMotor neuronMuscleHand withdraws

The spinal cord coordinates the immediate withdrawal. Information also reaches the brain, allowing the person to become aware of pain.

Across a synapse

  1. An electrical impulse reaches the presynaptic axon terminal.
  2. Vesicles release a neurotransmitter into the synaptic gap.
  3. The neurotransmitter diffuses and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  4. A new electrical impulse may begin in the next neuron.
Direction memory aid

Afferent approaches the CNS. Efferent exits the CNS.

06

Salinity and osmoregulation

Water movement across cell membranes

Osmoregulation controls water and solute concentrations inside an organism. Most seawater has a salinity close to 35 parts per thousand, although salinity changes near river mouths and where evaporation is high.

Diffusion

The net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Osmosis

The net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from higher water concentration to lower water concentration.

Isotonic solution
The solution has the same effective solute concentration as the cell, so there is no net water movement.
Hypotonic solution
The solution has a lower solute concentration than the cell. Water moves into the cell.
Hypertonic solution
The solution has a higher solute concentration than the cell. Water moves out of the cell.
Osmotic pressure
The pressure needed to prevent the net movement of water by osmosis.

Marine bony fish

They tend to lose water and gain salts. They drink seawater and remove excess salts through the gills and kidneys.

Freshwater fish

They tend to gain water and lose salts. They release large amounts of dilute urine and actively take up ions through the gills.

07

Plant responses

Tropisms, seasonal change and defence

A tropism is directional growth in response to a stimulus. Positive tropism is growth towards the stimulus, while negative tropism is growth away from it.

Phototropism
Growth in response to light. Auxin can cause greater cell elongation on the shaded side of a shoot, bending it towards light.
Gravitropism
Growth in response to gravity. Roots normally show positive gravitropism, while shoots show negative gravitropism.
Hydrotropism
Growth in response to water availability. Roots often grow towards moister soil.
Thigmotropism
Growth in response to touch, such as a climbing plant wrapping around a support.
Chemotropism
Growth in response to a chemical concentration.

Seasonal responses

Photoperiod is the duration of daylight. Plants use light information and hormones to coordinate flowering, leaf fall and other seasonal events. The timing of recurring biological events is called phenology.

Physical defence

Thorns, bark, waxy surfaces and hairs can reduce feeding or limit tissue damage.

Chemical defence

Plants can make substances that taste unpleasant, irritate skin or interfere with an herbivore's development.

08

Immune defence and treatment

Barriers, immune cells and medicines

Lines of defence

  1. First line: skin, mucus and other barriers reduce pathogen entry.
  2. Second line: inflammation and phagocytes provide a rapid non-specific response.
  3. Third line: lymphocytes recognise specific antigens. B cells produce antibodies, while memory cells support a faster later response.

Antigens are molecules recognised as foreign by the immune system. An antibody has a binding site with a shape that matches a particular antigen.

Antibiotics

Treat bacterial infections. They do not kill viruses. Misuse can increase selection for resistant bacteria.

Antivirals

Reduce viral replication or interfere with a viral process. They usually target particular viruses.

Vaccines

Expose the immune system to antigens safely, leading to memory cells and a faster response after later exposure.

Herd protection

High vaccination levels can reduce transmission, helping protect people who cannot receive a particular vaccine.

Do not confuse these terms

Antigens are recognised as foreign. Antibodies are proteins made by B cells that bind to specific antigens.

Graphs and CER

How to earn marks from evidence
C

Claim

Give a direct answer to the question.

E

Evidence

Quote a figure or pattern with units.

R

Reasoning

Explain how the science connects the evidence to the claim.

Graph routine

  1. Read both axes and the key before interpreting the graph.
  2. State the pattern and name both variables.
  3. Quote data from the start and end of the pattern.
  4. Include the unit shown on the axis.
  5. Explain the result using feedback-loop science.

Claim: Blood glucose regulation became less effective at higher injected glucose concentrations.

Evidence: Blood glucose remained near 10 mmol dm-3 up to about 85 mmol dm-3 injected, then increased above this level.

Reasoning: The rising blood glucose suggests that the normal negative feedback response could no longer return the concentration to its earlier level.

10

Assessment practice: applied homeostasis

Evidence and feedback responses

Interpret means read a value or pattern from the information provided.

Analyse means make a claim and connect it to evidence. Evaluate means weigh supporting evidence against limitations before reaching a judgment.

Intravenous glucose investigation

Four rats received glucose by mouth and four received it directly into the blood. For the oral group, urine glucose stayed at 0 mmol dm-3, blood plasma glucose stayed at 10 mmol dm-3, and mortality stayed at 0% across the tested concentrations.

≈40 mmol dm-3Blood plasma glucose read from the IV graph at 135 mmol dm-3 injected
85–95 mmol dm-3Plasma is still at the 10 mmol dm-3 baseline at 85, then is about 12 at the next measured point, 95
75% mortalityAt 115 mmol dm-3 injected; urine glucose is about 30 and plasma glucose about 15
100% mortalityAt 145 mmol dm-3 injected; urine glucose is about 75 and plasma glucose about 52

Claim: regulation is working at lower loads

At 60 and 85 mmol dm-3 injected, plasma glucose remains near the 10 mmol dm-3 baseline. This supports the claim that negative feedback is maintaining balance.

Claim: regulation is overwhelmed at higher loads

Above 85 mmol dm-3, plasma and urine glucose rise. Mortality then rises sharply. The response can no longer return the internal condition to its earlier range.

Threshold wording

The graph does not show the exact threshold. It shows that balance is maintained at 85 mmol dm-3 and is visibly disturbed by the next measured concentration, 95 mmol dm-3. State the range instead of inventing a more precise value.

How to evaluate the conclusion

Supporting evidence

For IV rats, increasing glucose is associated with higher plasma and urine glucose. Mortality also rises. The 100% mortality at the highest tested concentration supports the claim that very high IV glucose was poorly tolerated by these rats.

Limitations

  • Only four rats were used for each feeding route.
  • Rat results cannot be transferred directly to humans.
  • Body size alone does not determine glucose tolerance.
  • Oral and IV glucose enter the body by different routes.

Supported judgment: The evidence supports a conclusion about the tested rats under these conditions. It does not support the claim that larger humans can tolerate high glucose concentrations.

Blood clotting is positive feedback

Blood vessel damagedPlatelets attachChemicals releasedMore platelets activatePlatelet plug forms

The response amplifies the original platelet activation. The cycle ends when the damaged area is sealed, so the clear endpoint prevents unlimited clot growth.

Applying homeostasis to an infected cut

  1. The skin barrier is broken, allowing pathogens to enter. Clotting limits blood loss and helps seal the wound.
  2. Inflammatory chemicals widen nearby blood vessels and increase permeability. This produces redness and swelling while immune cells enter the tissue.
  3. Phagocytes engulf pathogens and dead cells. Pus contains dead immune cells and microbes suspended in tissue fluid.
  4. A fever can slow some pathogens and increase immune activity. Negative feedback later returns body temperature towards its normal range.

A

Knowing and understanding

Scientific knowledge, problem solving and judgments

Maximum: 8

At the end of Year 5, students should be able to:

  1. Explain scientific knowledge.
  2. Apply scientific knowledge and understanding to solve problems set in familiar and unfamiliar situations.
  3. Analyse and evaluate information to make scientifically supported judgments.

Level 0

The student does not reach a standard identified by any of the descriptors below.

Levels 1–2

The student is able to:

  1. State scientific knowledge.
  2. Apply scientific knowledge and understanding to suggest solutions to problems set in familiar situations.
  3. Interpret information to make judgments.

Levels 3–4

The student is able to:

  1. Outline scientific knowledge.
  2. Apply scientific knowledge and understanding to solve problems set in familiar situations.
  3. Interpret information to make scientifically supported judgments.

Levels 5–6

The student is able to:

  1. Describe scientific knowledge.
  2. Apply scientific knowledge and understanding to solve problems set in familiar situations and suggest solutions to problems set in unfamiliar situations.
  3. Analyse information to make scientifically supported judgments.

Levels 7–8

The student is able to:

  1. Explain scientific knowledge.
  2. Apply scientific knowledge and understanding to solve problems set in familiar and unfamiliar situations.
  3. Analyse and evaluate information to make scientifically supported judgments.
B

Inquiring and designing

Questions, hypotheses, variables and methods

Maximum: 8

At the end of Year 5, students should be able to:

  1. Explain a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
  2. Formulate a testable hypothesis and explain it using scientific reasoning.
  3. Explain how to manipulate the variables, and explain how data will be collected.
  4. Design scientific investigations.

Level 0

The student does not reach a standard identified by any of the descriptors below.

Levels 1–2

The student is able to:

  1. State a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
  2. Outline a testable hypothesis.
  3. Outline the variables.
  4. Design a method, with limited success.

Levels 3–4

The student is able to:

  1. Outline a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
  2. Formulate a testable hypothesis using scientific reasoning.
  3. Outline how to manipulate the variables, and outline how relevant data will be collected.
  4. Design a safe method in which they select materials and equipment.

Levels 5–6

The student is able to:

  1. Describe a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
  2. Formulate and explain a testable hypothesis using scientific reasoning.
  3. Describe how to manipulate the variables, and describe how sufficient, relevant data will be collected.
  4. Design a complete and safe method in which they select appropriate materials and equipment.

Levels 7–8

The student is able to:

  1. Explain a problem or question to be tested by a scientific investigation.
  2. Formulate and explain a testable hypothesis using correct scientific reasoning.
  3. Explain how to manipulate the variables, and explain how sufficient, relevant data will be collected.
  4. Design a logical, complete and safe method in which they select appropriate materials and equipment.
C

Processing and evaluating

Data, results, validity and improvements

Maximum: 8

At the end of Year 5, students should be able to:

  1. Present collected and transformed data.
  2. Interpret data and explain results using scientific reasoning.
  3. Evaluate the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of the scientific investigation.
  4. Evaluate the validity of the method.
  5. Explain improvements or extensions to the method.

Level 0

The student does not reach a standard identified by any of the descriptors below.

Levels 1–2

The student is able to:

  1. Collect and present data in numerical and/or visual forms.
  2. Interpret data.
  3. State the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  4. State the validity of the method based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  5. State improvements or extensions to the method.

Levels 3–4

The student is able to:

  1. Correctly collect and present data in numerical and/or visual forms.
  2. Accurately interpret data and explain results.
  3. Outline the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  4. Outline the validity of the method based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  5. Outline improvements or extensions to the method that would benefit the scientific investigation.

Levels 5–6

The student is able to:

  1. Correctly collect, organize and present data in numerical and/or visual forms.
  2. Accurately interpret data and explain results using scientific reasoning.
  3. Discuss the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  4. Discuss the validity of the method based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  5. Describe improvements or extensions to the method that would benefit the scientific investigation.

Levels 7–8

The student is able to:

  1. Correctly collect, organize, transform and present data in numerical and/or visual forms.
  2. Accurately interpret data and explain results using correct scientific reasoning.
  3. Evaluate the validity of a hypothesis based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  4. Evaluate the validity of the method based on the outcome of a scientific investigation.
  5. Explain improvements or extensions to the method that would benefit the scientific investigation.
D

Reflecting on the impacts of science

Applications, implications, language and sources

Maximum: 8

At the end of Year 5, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue.
  2. Discuss and evaluate the various implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue.
  3. Apply scientific language effectively.
  4. Document the work of others and sources of information used.

Level 0

The student does not reach a standard identified by any of the descriptors below.

Levels 1–2

The student is able to:

  1. Outline the ways in which science is used to address a specific problem or issue.
  2. Outline the implications of using science to solve a specific problem or issue, interacting with a factor.
  3. Apply scientific language to communicate understanding but does so with limited success.
  4. Document sources, with limited success.

Levels 3–4

The student is able to:

  1. Summarize the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue.
  2. Describe the implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue, interacting with a factor.
  3. Sometimes apply scientific language to communicate understanding.
  4. Sometimes document sources correctly.

Levels 5–6

The student is able to:

  1. Describe the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue.
  2. Discuss the implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue, interacting with a factor.
  3. Usually apply scientific language to communicate understanding clearly and precisely.
  4. Usually document sources correctly.

Levels 7–8

The student is able to:

  1. Explain the ways in which science is applied and used to address a specific problem or issue.
  2. Discuss and evaluate the implications of using science and its application to solve a specific problem or issue, interacting with a factor.
  3. Consistently apply scientific language to communicate understanding clearly and precisely.
  4. Document sources completely.
